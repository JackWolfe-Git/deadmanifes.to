Philosophy of the Deadman

Core Tenets for a path forward

1. Use the tools.

AI is here, and it's already capable of an absurd amount.

We're still early enough that the people using these systems now have some say in what they become. I think that's worth participating in.

Choosing not to use them is completely valid. But it also means voluntarily limiting what you can do with technology.

2. Make ideas real.

We're past the novelty stage.

Give someone an idea, access to the right tools and enough persistence, and they can now build things that would previously have required a team of specialists.

The gap between having an idea and being able to make it has become much smaller.

3. Be honest about the process.

If I use AI to make something, I'll say I used AI.

There's a difference between building something entirely through your own expertise, using AI as an assistant, and effectively getting an AI to build the thing for you.

Those are different processes. There's no reason to pretend otherwise.

Using AI also doesn't remove responsibility for the result. That means checking its work, thinking about attribution and making sure that something being generated doesn't become an excuse to pass someone else's work off as your own.

4. Learn what you're using.

AI isn't magic and it isn't automatically correct.

If I'm going to rely on these systems, I should understand enough to question them, check their work and recognise when they're wrong.

The more capable they become, the less useful blind trust becomes.

5. Build, don't just generate.

I'm interested in using AI to actually make things.

Websites. Software. Music tools. Personal systems. Weird experiments. Things I need. Things that probably shouldn't exist but are interesting enough to build anyway.

The point isn't an endless stream of disposable content.

It's to turn ideas into things that work.

6. Keep the individual in the loop.

I want technology to give individuals more control over what they can create, understand and do with their own technology.

I'm particularly interested in tools that make one person more capable, rather than simply making existing institutions more efficient.

7. Don't ignore the physical cost.

AI isn't weightless.

The computers, data centres, electricity, cooling and hardware behind these systems have real environmental and material costs.

I don't think that means we shouldn't use them. I think it means we should stop pretending the computation is free and be conscious about what we're actually using it for.

8. Build for what comes next.

AI is making complex technical work accessible to people who previously couldn't have done it.

That has obvious creative benefits, but it also changes the security landscape. The same tools that lower the barrier to building something can lower the barrier to breaking it.

They can also lower the barrier to fixing it.

An individual can increasingly use AI to understand an unfamiliar codebase, design a system, configure infrastructure or work through a technical problem from the ground up.

I don't think we should assume that today's digital infrastructure is permanent.

If parts of it eventually have to be rebuilt, I want ordinary technically curious people to be capable of participating in that process.

My Vision for AI Usage

As I write this on 3rd October 2026, I think we're still in the very early stages of the AI era.

The technology is already undeniably capable. What's less clear is what we're actually going to do with it.

What interests me is the change in scale of what one person can realistically attempt.

You can have an idea in the morning and potentially have a working version of it by the evening. You can ask questions about programming, design, writing, research or technical problems without already being an expert in every field involved.

That doesn't make expertise worthless. It changes where expertise matters.

You still need judgement. You still need taste. You still need to know what you're trying to achieve. You need to recognise when something is wrong, decide what to keep, throw things away and keep going when the first attempt doesn't work.

But the old barrier of I don't know how to do that is becoming less absolute.

You can ask.

Then you can try.

Then you can learn from what happened and try again.

That's a significant increase in individual creative capability, and I think we should take advantage of it.

I'm also not interested in pretending that AI wasn't involved.

If I spend years learning how to build something myself, that's one process. If I use AI to help me build something I've never built before, that's another. If I ask an AI to produce something and barely touch the result, that's another again.

I don't think any of those processes need to be morally dressed up as something they're not.

The important thing is being honest about what happened and taking responsibility for the result.

That includes plagiarism and attribution. If an AI produces something based on someone else's work, calling it AI-generated doesn't make the underlying problem disappear. The tool doesn't absolve me of responsibility for what I publish.

The same applies to accuracy.

AI can be extremely useful and completely wrong in the same conversation.

Using it properly means knowing that.

There's a bigger reason I want to engage with these systems beyond making my own work easier.

We're at an unusually early point in the development of a technology that is likely to change how people interact with computers.

I don't want that relationship to be designed entirely around the needs of the largest companies or institutions.

If the technology can make an individual more capable, I want to explore that.

If it can help someone build their own tools, understand systems they couldn't previously access, or create something that would otherwise have remained an idea, I want to explore that too.

And if it can eventually help someone rebuild a piece of digital infrastructure from the ground up, that matters just as much.

I don't know whether the infrastructure we've built will collapse, fragment or simply change beyond recognition. I don't think anyone can know that with certainty.

But I do think it's worth preparing for a world where rebuilding complex digital systems is no longer an impossible task reserved for enormous organisations.

That possibility cuts both ways.

The same accessibility can create new security problems. It can also create new forms of resilience.

I'd rather understand the tools well enough to be useful on either side of that equation.

That's what Deadmanifesto is really about.

Not worshipping AI.

Not rejecting it.

Not pretending it does everything.

Not pretending it does nothing.

Using it, understanding it, being honest about it, and seeing what one person can do with it.

I don't want to watch the AI era happen. I want to participate in it.
